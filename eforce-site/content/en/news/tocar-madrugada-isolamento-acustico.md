Headphones on, module at a comfortable level, two in the morning. On your side of the headphones the kit is quiet. On your downstairs neighbor's side, every kick hit lands as a dry knock in the ceiling. That gap is the actual problem with practicing at odd hours, and turning the volume down does not solve it — volume was never the source.

## Why the neighbor hears your kit while you are wearing headphones

Sound leaves your kit by two different paths, and those paths ask for opposite solutions.

The first is airborne noise: the stick tip clicking on the mesh, the crack of a rim shot, the leakage out of your headphones, a monitor left running. It travels through air, loses energy fast with distance, and gets blocked well by a wall, a closed door or absorbent material. It is the noise most people try to fix, and it is the smaller of the two problems.

The second is impact noise, also called structure-borne noise. When the beater hits the kick pad, part of the mechanical energy of that hit never becomes sound in the air: it goes straight into the pedal frame, through the subfloor and out across the slab. Concrete carries vibration far better than air carries sound, and it does so with very little loss along the way. That is why the neighbor below usually complains before the neighbor through the wall, and why they describe it as a rhythmic thud rather than as music.

Mesh pads already handle a good share of the airborne side — the weave flexes and turns the hit into movement instead of a crack, which is why [the whole EF2 line uses mesh on snare, toms and kick](/en/kits/ef2-v1). What mesh cannot do is stop vibration from running down the rack and the pedal frame. That part comes down to how the kit sits on the floor.

## What each piece of the kit sends into the slab

Splitting the sources is worth doing before spending money on materials, because they do not contribute equally.

The kick pedal is the worst offender by a wide margin. It concentrates three sources at one spot: the beater striking the pad, your foot dropping onto the footboard, and the metal pedal frame resting directly on the floor. That is low-frequency energy entering the structure exactly where the structure conducts best.

The hi-hat pedal repeats the same mechanism with less mass behind it, but at a much higher repetition rate — in a sixteenth-note groove it hits more times per bar than the kick does.

Snare and tom pads contribute less, and they contribute indirectly: the hit climbs the mounting arm, runs along the rack tubing and comes back down through the stand feet. A rack with rubber feet sitting on a cold tile floor transmits more than most players assume.

Then there is everything nobody counts: a creaking throne, your left foot tapping time on the floor away from the pedal, and your own body shifting on a floating wood floor.

## Does a rug under the kit actually help?

It helps, less than it promises. A thick rug or a carpet mat cuts airborne noise inside the room, protects the floor and damps the higher-frequency impact coming off the rack feet. For the kick hit it behaves like an undersized shock absorber: the energy passes through the thickness of the rug and keeps loading into the slab.

Soft foam on its own is misleading too. Under the weight of the kit plus your own, low-density foam compresses to the end of its travel and starts transmitting vibration as if it were a rigid block. The material has to stay elastic while loaded — the load decides which density makes sense, not how soft the foam feels in your hand.

## How a floating platform works

The principle comes from basic mechanics: mass sitting on an elastic element forms a mass-spring system, and that system attenuates everything above its own resonant frequency. In practice you build a heavy, rigid platform floating on an elastic layer, and the assembly filters vibration before it reaches the floor.

A build that works usually has three parts. On top, a rigid platform — two sheets of MDF bonded with a damping compound between them, which dissipates energy instead of letting both sheets ring together. In the middle, the elastic layer: high-density polyurethane foam rated for load, dense rubber blocks or compressed mineral wool, chosen for the weight it will carry. Underneath, no side contact at all: if the platform touches a baseboard, a wall or a door sill, vibration finds a rigid shortcut and the rest of the build stops mattering.

The best-known DIY version, with cut tennis balls sandwiched between two sheets, works in principle — it cuts contact area sharply and damps well — but it raises the kit by several centimeters, which forces the throne up too and throws off your pedal posture. If you go that route, plan the seat height along with it rather than afterwards.

Two quick corrections often pay off more than any platform: swapping a hard plastic beater for soft felt or foam, and putting the pedal frame on an elastic block independent from the rack. A softer beater lowers the impact peak on the mesh and treats the pad fabric better over time.

## What to adjust on the kit before building anything

A lot of the extra noise comes from compensating for bad settings. If trigger sensitivity is low, you hit harder without noticing to get the same response — and more force means more energy heading into the slab. Raising sensitivity and recalibrating the threshold usually drops hand and foot effort without costing you dynamics. The [F10 module lets you set sensitivity and response curve per piece](/en/technology), which makes that adjustment quite granular.

Mesh tension belongs to the same calculation. Overtightened mesh returns a dry rebound and raises the sharp click of the stick tip; loose mesh sinks, delays the sensor reading and makes you compensate with force. The useful spot sits in between, and it is worth revisiting sensitivity after any tension change, since one shifts the other.

If crosstalk or phantom hits show up after you build the floating platform, that is not a coincidence: the platform changes how vibration circulates between pads. Go through the settings piece by piece, and if you get stuck somewhere, [talk to E-Force support](/en/support) before swapping cables and sensors blind.

## What is left after all of it

With mesh pads, a soft beater, a decoupled pedal and a rack on an elastic base, what remains during quiet hours is the stick clicking on the weave — a short sound with no sustain that dies inside your own room. At that point the limit stops being your neighbor and becomes your ears: closed headphones at a moderate level carry a long session, open headphones cranked wear you out in half an hour.
